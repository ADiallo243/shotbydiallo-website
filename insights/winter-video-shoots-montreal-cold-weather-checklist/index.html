Winter Video Shoots in Montréal: A Cold-Weather Production Checklist

A practical field plan for batteries, snow, changing temperatures, sound, crew comfort and a reliable backup option.

Published November 27, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

Winter scenes can give a business video a clear sense of place, but cold changes the plan for both equipment and people. A battery that lasts through a summer interview may not behave the same way on an outdoor Montréal shoot; moving a cold camera straight into a warm room can also fog equipment. Build a shorter, protected exterior plan with a warm reset point and an indoor alternative before the crew arrives.

1. Start with the actual forecast and equipment limits

Check the forecast for the shoot location and the hours you expect to be outside. Temperature alone does not describe the whole day: wind, precipitation, ground conditions and the amount of time the crew will be exposed all affect the plan. If conditions are unsafe for a person or beyond the operating guidance for a camera, lens, recorder or battery, move the work indoors or reschedule.

Check the manual for each camera and accessory rather than assuming that a weather-sealed body is waterproof or suitable for every temperature. Write down the limits that matter, confirm any required rain or snow covers, and keep a dry, sheltered place for changing batteries or media. Equipment-specific instructions take priority over generic winter tips.

2. Plan for shorter takes and warm batteries

Cold can reduce battery performance. Sony notes that low temperature can shorten battery life, particularly during extended video recording, and recommends keeping batteries warm before use. Nikon likewise advises carrying a spare in an inside pocket and swapping it in when needed.[1][2]

  • Arrive with charged, labelled batteries and at least one spare per camera system, subject to the manufacturer’s guidance.
  • Keep spares insulated in an inner pocket or a suitable warm case; rotate them when a camera battery runs low.
  • Use a concise shot list. Capture the essential wide, medium and detail shots first, then use remaining time for optional moments.
  • Set a planned warm-up point so the schedule does not depend on staying outside continuously.

For a business story, useful exterior coverage may be just the storefront, a recognizable street approach, a few establishing shots and one or two short action moments. Capture interviews, longer dialogue and any shots that require lens or rig changes in a controlled indoor setup.

3. Protect the camera from snow and temperature swings

Use the camera’s hood and a model-compatible rain or snow cover when conditions call for it. Nikon recommends shielding equipment from precipitation and avoiding lens changes outdoors unless necessary. Keep a dry cloth and a separate bag for wet outer covers so moisture is not carried into the next setup.[2]

When moving from a cold exterior into a heated vehicle or building, do not immediately expose the gear to warm, humid air. Nikon’s guidance is to put the camera in a bag and let it warm gradually; this helps reduce the chance of fogging. Follow the instructions for your specific model if condensation appears or if its manual recommends a different procedure.[2]

Keep the sequence deliberate: finish the exterior take, remove snow or surface moisture as directed by the manufacturer, close the camera bag, and let the gear acclimate before opening it indoors. Avoid placing cold equipment beside a heater.

4. Make sound and continuity part of the winter plan

Wind, scarves, hoods and bulky coats can all affect where a microphone can sit and how a person moves on camera. Record a short sound check at the location, use a windscreen appropriate to the microphone, and monitor with headphones. If clean dialogue is central to the story, record it indoors and use the exterior for atmosphere and silent action.

Snow and winter clothing also change continuity. Note which coats, hats, gloves and scarves belong to each person, whether snow is falling, and which direction the subject faces. Capture a few seconds of room tone or exterior ambience, plus clean details of the entrance, signage and activity. These notes help an editor join footage captured during brief weather windows.

5. Schedule crew comfort and a stop point

Cold affects people as well as batteries. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety recommends planning work-break schedules and taking breaks in a warm area; its cold-environment guidance also discusses recognizing cold stress and frostbite. Build a warm shelter into the call sheet, make breaks part of the schedule, and stop or relocate when conditions exceed the production’s safe plan.[3][4]

This article is a production-planning checklist, not a workplace safety assessment. Employers and production leads should check applicable occupational health and safety requirements, site rules and weather alerts for the actual job. Do not keep filming through numbness, persistent shivering, confusion or other concerning symptoms; move to a warm place and follow appropriate first-aid and medical procedures.

6. Keep an indoor version of the story ready

Choose an indoor backup that still advances the brief: a customer-service moment near the entrance, a product detail, a staff portrait, a short interview or a sequence of work in progress. Confirm access and permissions ahead of time. If weather closes the exterior option, the crew can make the call without inventing a new plan on the day.

A simple winter call-sheet note can include:

  • Forecast check time and decision-maker;
  • Exterior location and protected equipment setup;
  • Warm break location and accessible route back indoors;
  • Battery rotation and camera acclimation plan;
  • Core exterior shots, listed in priority order;
  • Indoor backup shots and any permissions needed;
  • Conditions that trigger a pause, relocation or reschedule.

Winter shoot checklist

  • Model manuals and operating-temperature guidance checked;
  • Weather, wind and precipitation reviewed for the planned hours;
  • Charged spare batteries labelled and kept warm;
  • Compatible camera protection, lens hood and dry cloths packed;
  • Cold-to-warm equipment transition planned;
  • Short exterior shot list prioritized;
  • Microphone wind protection and sound-check plan confirmed;
  • Warm shelter, breaks and stop-work conditions agreed;
  • Indoor backup location and permissions confirmed.

For other parts of the production plan, review the video location scout checklist, the lighting checklist and the audio checklist. If the shoot uses public space in Montréal, confirm the applicable location and permit requirements in the permit checklist.
