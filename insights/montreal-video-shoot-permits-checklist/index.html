Montréal Video Shoot Permits: A Business Production Checklist

Identify the location, public-space impact and approval path before the crew, equipment and schedule are locked.

Published November 5, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A simple interview inside a client’s office and a commercial that reserves curb space are not the same production. The permit question depends on where the crew works, what it occupies and which municipal services are affected. This checklist helps a Montréal business identify the right questions early; it is general production guidance, not legal advice or a substitute for confirmation from the City, borough, property owner or regulator.

Begin with a location-and-impact map

Before requesting quotes or fixing the shoot date, list every location and the activity planned there. Separate private interiors, private exteriors, streets and sidewalks, parks and public places, municipal buildings, transit property and aerial work. Add the equipment footprint, vehicles, crew size, hours, sound, lighting, generators, parking needs and any interruption to pedestrian, bicycle or road traffic.

This map prevents a common planning error: treating “we are filming in Montréal” as one approval question. The City states that an application is required for each public place occupied during filming, including roads, alleys, sidewalks, parks, public places and municipal buildings.[1] A production with three public locations may therefore require separate location-specific information rather than one general authorization.

Private property permission and public-property permits are different

For a private office, restaurant, residence or commercial property, obtain written permission from the person authorized to grant access. The agreement should identify the permitted areas, dates and hours; loading and parking; power use; noise limits; restoration; logos or artwork visible on camera; insurance requirements; and who can stop or change activity on site.

That private agreement does not authorize occupation of the street outside. A production vehicle, lighting stand, cable path, reserved parking area or sidewalk setup may affect the public domain even when the scene is inside. Montréal’s public-property occupancy guidance explains that temporary occupation can involve streets, sidewalks and parking, and that charges depend on factors such as occupied dimensions, road type, transit routes, bicycle facilities and parking meters.[2]

Confirm the responsible borough and process

The City’s filming-permit page is location-sensitive: procedures, documents, lead times and fees vary by borough and type of place. Its general path directs productions to contact the Bureau du cinéma et de la télévision de Montréal to open a file, then complete the applicable application. The same page also identifies borough-specific exceptions—for example, Verdun directs filming applications through the borough rather than the BCTM.[1]

Use the exact address to confirm the borough, then read the current instructions for that location. Do not copy a fee or deadline from another borough. The City page shows examples ranging from applications at least 10 business days before filming to processing windows of 15 business days, while other local procedures differ. Build contingency time for questions, revisions, engineering documents, resident notices and payment.

Prepare a complete production file

For corporate video and web projects, Montréal’s official guidance lists documents such as liability insurance naming the City as co-insured, a synopsis, shooting locations, production vehicles, schedule and crew list. For advertising, video-clip and professional photography work, it lists a storyboard or script along with locations, vehicles and schedule. Required insurance amounts vary across borough instructions on the same City page, so the certificate should match the specific authority’s current requirement.[1]

A practical file should also include:

  • a one-page production summary and on-site contact;
  • the precise address and map for every location;
  • arrival, setup, filming, meal, strike and departure times;
  • crew, cast and vehicle counts;
  • equipment placed outdoors or in circulation paths;
  • power, lighting, sound and generator details;
  • parking or curb-space requests;
  • accessibility and emergency routes;
  • property permissions and special stakeholder approvals;
  • a weather alternative and cancellation decision time.

The business video brief can capture the communication goals and approvals; the permit file translates the creative plan into physical activity at each location.

Plan street, sidewalk and parking impacts precisely

If the production occupies public property, Montréal may require a layout plan. Street closures or other traffic changes may require a signage or engineering plan. The City also notes that written approvals may be needed from affected stakeholders such as owners, school boards or the STM, and that neighbouring residents may need filming notices for certain applications.[1]

Draw what the permit reviewer needs to understand: camera position, equipment footprint, vehicle placement, cable routing, clear pedestrian width, bicycle or vehicle detour, loading, fire access and the limits of any reserved area. Keep the approved plan available on set and brief the crew before unloading. An approved public-domain permit still comes with operating obligations; Montréal reminds permit holders to follow municipal rules after issuance.[3]

Treat drones as a separate aviation decision

A property owner’s permission or municipal filming permit does not replace federal aviation requirements. Transport Canada’s rules depend on the aircraft and operation. Its current guidance says drones under 250 grams do not need registration or marking, but operators must still follow the rules for microdrones. Other operations can require registration, a pilot certificate and compliance with operating and airspace limits.[4]

Before promising aerial footage, verify the exact drone, pilot qualification, airspace, distance from bystanders, site restrictions and any additional authorization. If the flight cannot be confirmed safely and legally, replace it with a ground-based establishing shot rather than allowing an unverified aerial plan to control the schedule.

Escalate special effects and unusual activity early

Smoke, flame, pyrotechnics, simulated weapons, stunts, wet-downs and other effects can involve separate safety review. Montréal’s filming page states that special effects require a permit from the Service de sécurité incendie de Montréal at least five days before filming.[1] That minimum does not replace the longer lead time that the location or borough may require.

Describe unusual activity at the first permit conversation. Do not hide an effect inside a generic “commercial shoot” description and reveal it on set. The production schedule should include the qualified specialists, safety perimeter, emergency plan and restoration work required by the authority and location.

Keep the creative plan flexible until approvals are confirmed

A permit-dependent shot should not be the only way to communicate the message. Identify a smaller-footprint alternative for each complicated setup: shoot from private property instead of occupying a sidewalk, use a handheld or compact lighting plan, replace a street closure with a controlled interior, or capture a ground-level alternative to a drone shot.

This flexibility also protects the budget. Public-property fees can include application, issuance, occupied area, lane obstruction, parking-meter or location charges, and the figures vary by borough. Use the current official page for the chosen address and request an estimate when the process allows it.[1]

Pre-production permit checklist

  • Every location has an exact address, owner or authority and planned activity.
  • Private-property permission is documented by an authorized person.
  • Public-domain impacts include crew, equipment, vehicles, parking and circulation.
  • The responsible borough and current process have been confirmed.
  • Each public place has its own application where required.
  • The insurance certificate matches the authority’s current wording and amount.
  • Synopsis, storyboard or script, schedule, locations, vehicles and crew information are ready.
  • Layout, signage or engineering plans are included when applicable.
  • Resident and stakeholder notices or approvals are planned.
  • Drone and special-effects requirements are reviewed separately.
  • The budget includes current permit and occupancy charges.
  • The shot list has lower-impact alternatives if an approval changes.
  • The final permit conditions are briefed to the crew and available on set.

Final planning note

Permit planning is not paperwork added after the creative decisions. It is part of location design, scheduling, safety and client approval. Start with the physical footprint of the production, verify the correct authority for each address, and keep the creative approach flexible until written permissions are in place. For a multi-location or unusual shoot, confirm the current requirements directly with the City, borough, property owner and any other responsible regulator.
