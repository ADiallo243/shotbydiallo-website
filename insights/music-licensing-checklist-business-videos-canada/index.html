Music Licensing for Business Videos in Canada: A Practical Checklist

Choose the track, intended uses and documentation before the edit—not when a platform flags the finished video.

Published November 7, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial7 min read

Music can make a business video feel finished, but finding a track online, buying a copy or adding a credit does not automatically grant permission to use it in advertising or branded content. The safe workflow is to define every intended use, identify the rights involved, obtain an appropriate licence and keep the evidence with the project files.

Start with the intended uses

List where the video will appear before selecting music: the company website, organic social posts, paid ads, YouTube, TikTok, presentations, events, broadcast or another channel. Also record the territory, term, edit lengths and whether the client may reuse or adapt the video. A licence that covers one platform or organic post may not cover paid media, another territory or a future campaign.

This is general production guidance, not legal advice. Music rights and contracts vary. Ask the rights holder, licensing service or a qualified lawyer when the scope is unclear.

Do not confuse access with permission

Canada’s intellectual-property guidance distinguishes ownership of a copy from ownership of copyright: purchasing a sound recording does not itself transfer the copyright. Copyright can protect musical works, performances and sound recordings, and a written licence can describe how a protected work may be used.[1][2]

Downloading a track, subscribing to a streaming service or hearing the music inside a social app is therefore not enough evidence for a commercial video. Treat the licence text—not the download button or song availability—as the source of truth.

Check the composition and the recording

A song and a particular recording of that song can involve separate interests. Canadian government guidance states that copyright protects musical works and also protects performances and sound recordings.[1] Confirm that the permission you receive covers the music and the specific recording used in the edit.

SOCAN describes synchronization as the use of music with visual content and provides a synchronization-licence request that asks about uses including advertising and corporate video, territory and licence duration.[3][4] That form is useful evidence of the questions a production team should settle, but it does not mean SOCAN controls every track or every right.

Read stock-music terms for the real campaign

“Royalty-free” does not mean copyright-free or unrestricted. Review the actual licence for commercial use, client work, paid advertising, social platforms, websites, broadcast, geographic limits, audience or impression limits, sublicensing and whether the licence remains valid after a subscription ends.

Save the licence, invoice, track title, artist, download date and project registration. If a service requires the project or channel to be registered, complete that step before publication. Keep the original audio file and documentation together so a future editor can identify the approved track.

Treat platform libraries as platform-specific

TikTok says content promoting a brand, product or service should use its Commercial Music Library because its licences for music outside that library do not cover commercial use in branded content. If music comes from outside the library, TikTok requires confirmation that the user has obtained the necessary licences.[5]

YouTube says its Audio Library contains royalty-free production music and sound effects for videos on YouTube, while also warning that it cannot give legal guidance about music use off the platform.[6] Do not assume that music cleared inside one platform can be exported into a universal master for websites, ads and other apps.

Credit is a condition, not a substitute

Some licences require attribution and specify the exact wording or placement. YouTube’s guidance for Creative Commons Attribution content lists the title, author, source URL and licence information as useful credit details.[7] But credit does not create permission where none exists, and a “no infringement intended” disclaimer does not replace a licence.

Plan for paid ads and alternate edits

Tell the music provider when the video will be promoted as an ad. Confirm whether cutdowns, alternate aspect ratios, different voiceovers, translations and future revisions remain within scope. The Google Ads video creative checklist can help identify the required versions before the licence is finalized.

When the campaign needs several lengths, document whether the licence covers all edits as one project or requires separate registrations. Avoid changing tracks late in post-production unless the replacement is reviewed against the same intended-use list.

Archive a rights packet with delivery

Create one folder containing the final licence, invoice, track and artist details, rights holder or supplier, approved platforms, territory, term, attribution wording and any project registration. Add a short readme describing restrictions. The client should know what was licensed, for which project and when renewal or additional permission may be needed.

Music clearance checklist

  • Every intended platform, paid use, territory and term is listed.
  • The exact track and recording are identified.
  • The licence covers commercial client work and the planned campaign.
  • Composition and recording permissions are addressed where required.
  • Platform-library music is not assumed to be portable elsewhere.
  • Paid advertising, cutdowns and alternate versions are covered.
  • Attribution requirements are copied exactly.
  • The licence, invoice and download evidence are saved.
  • Project or channel registration is completed when required.
  • Restrictions and renewal dates are included in the client handoff.
  • Unclear rights are reviewed by the provider or qualified counsel before publication.
