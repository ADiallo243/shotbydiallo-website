Video Location Scouting: A Practical Checklist for Business Shoots

Inspect a filming location for creative fit, safe operations and reliable production before the crew and cameras arrive.

Published November 16, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial9 min read

A location may look perfect in a photo and still create serious production problems. A useful scout tests the real conditions of the shoot: access, activity, hazards, sound, light, power, permissions and the route from arrival to wrap. The result should be a written plan that the client, location contact and production team can all use.

Start with the scenes, not the architecture

Bring the approved brief and a preliminary shot list. For every scene, identify who appears, what they do, what must be visible and how much space the camera, lighting and sound team need. A beautiful room is not the right room if the key action cannot be staged clearly.

Walk the location in story order. Mark the intended background, subject position, camera position and likely movement. Take reference photos from the actual camera angles rather than only wide promotional views. Connect each setup to the business video brief so location choices stay tied to the communication goal.

Scout at the time the shoot will happen

Traffic, customers, deliveries, sunlight and mechanical noise change throughout the day. Whenever practical, visit during the same hours and weekday planned for filming. Ask what will be different on the shoot date, including construction, events, maintenance, seasonal displays and staff schedules.

Record the direction and movement of daylight at each setup. Note when direct sun reaches windows, whether nearby signs turn on and whether exterior light will change faster than the scene can be filmed. If timing cannot be matched, document the uncertainty and build a lighting or schedule alternative.

Confirm control of the space

Identify the person authorized to approve filming and the exact areas included. Confirm access hours, setup and wrap time, noise restrictions, maximum occupancy, furniture rules, wall or ceiling restrictions, cleaning expectations and any required security or building staff.

List spaces that remain operational. A business may need to serve customers, protect confidential work or keep an emergency route open. Agree on how filming zones will be separated, how interruptions will be handled and who can pause production if operations require it.

Separate private permission from public permits

Written permission from a property contact does not automatically cover a sidewalk, street, alley, park, municipal building or reserved parking area. Determine where every tripod, light, cable, vehicle and crew member will stand.

The City of Montréal requires an application for each public place occupied for filming and lists documents such as proof of liability insurance, locations, vehicles, schedule and crew. A layout plan is required when public property is occupied, and additional requirements apply to street closures, drones and special effects.[1] Use the complete Montréal filming permit checklist when any setup extends beyond private property.

Inspect hazards as part of the production plan

A scout should record more than creative obstacles. Look for stairs, uneven floors, low ceilings, wet areas, traffic, machinery, hot surfaces, stored materials, working at height, public movement, restricted exits and anything that could change once equipment and cables are introduced.

The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety describes hazard identification as finding and recording possible hazards, analyzing the associated risk and choosing ways to eliminate or control it. It recommends considering the physical environment, equipment, materials, task steps and non-routine activities, while involving people familiar with the work area.[2]

Ask the site contact about hazards that are not obvious during the visit. Record the issue, exact location, responsible person and required control. A location photo is useful, but it should support—not replace—a written note.

Protect exits, fire equipment and public routes

Map crew circulation before placing equipment. Emergency exits, extinguishers, alarms, electrical panels, accessible routes and staff work paths must remain usable. Decide where cases, coats, carts and waste will go; “temporarily” storing them in a corridor often becomes an all-day obstruction.

CCOHS recommends that workplace inspections consider people, environment, equipment, materials and process, including areas such as parking, storage and rest spaces.[3] Adapt the scout to the actual site and work rather than relying on a generic room checklist.

Plan load-in, parking and vertical access

Trace the route from the production vehicle to every setup. Measure narrow doors and elevators when large cases, carts or grip equipment are planned. Confirm loading-zone rules, elevator reservations, keys, badges, security check-in and the distance from parking.

Separate the arrival time from the first-camera time. Include unloading, sign-in, furniture moves, protection for floors and walls, and travel between levels. If public parking or a curb lane must be reserved, include that need in the permit and location plan rather than assuming an empty space will be available.

Test power instead of counting outlets

Photograph and label the outlets near each setup. Ask which circuits they use and what equipment already shares them. Do not infer electrical capacity from the number of receptacles or use an unverified panel. A qualified person should assess distribution when the load or site conditions require it.

Plan cable routes that avoid exits, customer paths and wet areas. Record where protection or overhead routing is needed. Battery operation can simplify a setup, but calculate realistic runtime, charging, spares and a safe charging location.

Listen for continuous and intermittent noise

Stand quietly at each interview or dialogue position and record a sample. Listen for ventilation, refrigerators, elevators, plumbing, music, traffic, aircraft, schools, neighbouring tenants, doors and room reverberation. Ask which sources can be paused and who has authority to pause them.

Intermittent noise is easy to miss during a short visit. Note delivery windows, bells, scheduled announcements and equipment cycles. Choose an alternate room or a sound plan before shoot day when a noise source cannot be controlled.

Check privacy, branding and sensitive information

Scan every likely frame for computer screens, paperwork, customer information, security systems, access codes, personal photographs, licence plates, artwork, third-party logos and people who have not agreed to appear. Decide what will be removed, covered, reframed or cleared.

Identify where participants can wait, change and review releases without blocking operations. For customers or employees appearing in testimonial-style scenes, use a documented consent process and the testimonial consent checklist.

Build a weather decision for exterior work

Record exposure to wind, rain, heat, cold and changing ground conditions. Identify shelter, a safe holding area, equipment protection and an indoor alternative. Set a decision time and name the person who will make the call.

Environment and Climate Change Canada says its weather alerts describe both the event and its expected impacts. Warnings, advisories and watches require different levels of action or readiness.[4] Check the official forecast and active alerts close to the shoot; a scout completed days earlier is not a weather clearance.

Create a usable scout package

Turn the visit into one shared document. Include the address, location contact, access window, parking and load-in notes, floor plan or layout, setup photos, sun and sound observations, power notes, hazards and controls, permit status, restricted areas, weather alternative and unanswered questions.

Assign every open item to a person and date. A photo folder without decisions leaves the crew to repeat the scout on shoot day. Review the package with the client and department leads, then carry the confirmed details into the video production call sheet.

Video location scout checklist

  • Every scene has a tested subject, camera and background position.
  • The scout matches the planned shoot time as closely as practical.
  • The authorized location contact and included areas are documented.
  • Operating hours, customers, deliveries and interruptions are planned.
  • Private permission and public-property permits are treated separately.
  • Hazards are recorded with their exact location, control and owner.
  • Emergency exits, fire equipment and accessible routes stay clear.
  • Parking, load-in, elevators, doors and security access are confirmed.
  • Power sources and cable routes have been assessed for the planned setup.
  • Continuous and intermittent noise has been tested at dialogue positions.
  • Daylight direction and practical lighting changes are documented.
  • Sensitive information, artwork, branding and bystanders are reviewed.
  • Participant holding, changing and consent logistics are assigned.
  • Exterior work has a weather threshold, decision time and fallback.
  • The scout package names owners and deadlines for every open question.
