Business Video Production Timeline: A Practical Montréal Planning Guide

Work backwards from the date your video needs to be ready. Put approvals, access and review time on the schedule—not just the shoot.

Published November 24, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A shoot may take one day; the project around it includes briefing, decisions, logistics, editing and review. There is no reliable universal turnaround for every business video. Scope, locations, language versions, stakeholder availability and revision rounds all affect the calendar. Use the workback plan below as a starting framework, then confirm the actual milestones with your producer.

Start with the delivery date and the exact deliverables

Define what “ready” means: final approved master, social cutdowns, captions, translations, licensed music, or files uploaded to a platform. Also decide whether “ready” means delivered to your team or publicly launched. Those dates can differ. A deliverables checklist helps turn a broad request for “a video” into a production scope.

Then work backwards from the date. If the video supports a campaign, event or opening, keep a buffer between receiving final files and the public deadline. That gives your team time to check spelling, links, captions, permissions and playback on the destination page.

A flexible workback schedule

This is an illustrative sequence, not a promised Montréal industry standard. A simple, single-location project with one decision-maker may move quickly; a multi-location shoot, several languages or many reviewers may need more time. Confirm each block against the actual scope.

  1. Kickoff and brief: agree on audience, goal, message, proof points, deliverables, budget assumptions and a single feedback owner. Identify any event date or publishing deadline now.
  2. Concept, script and approvals: develop the treatment or outline, confirm factual and brand review, and lock the language and call to action before production. Use the business video brief questions to surface decisions early.
  3. Pre-production: book the location and people, confirm access and permissions, build the shot plan, prepare wardrobe or products, and distribute the call sheet. Set a date for the client to approve the plan.
  4. Shoot: capture the agreed scenes and planned supporting footage. Leave time for setup, location transitions, speaker comfort and a short check that the essential shots are covered before wrapping.
  5. First edit and review: the producer assembles the agreed cut; the client gathers one consolidated set of notes from the people named at kickoff. Review against the approved brief and factual accuracy, not new preferences that were never scoped.
  6. Revisions, versions and delivery: complete the included revision rounds, prepare agreed aspect ratios or language versions, check captions and usage rights, and deliver the files listed in the proposal.
  7. Launch buffer: test the final export where it will be used, confirm names, dates, links and calls to action, and keep contingency time before the public deadline.

Plan milestones, not vague status labels

Replace “editing this week” with a milestone the team can act on: “producer sends first cut by Tuesday; client returns one consolidated note document by Friday; producer sends revised cut next Wednesday.” A named owner and a decision deadline make the handoff clear.

Set one person to collect internal feedback. Ask reviewers to flag timecode, what they want changed and why it better serves the approved goal. If factual, legal or technical claims need review, identify the right reviewer before filming. The script review checklist can help close those questions before they become edit-stage changes.

What changes the schedule?

  • Unconfirmed scope: adding scenes, deliverables or languages after the plan is approved can require new work.
  • People and location availability: executives, customers, talent, studios and operating businesses may have limited windows.
  • Approvals: several independent reviewers, slow feedback or conflicting notes can extend each review cycle.
  • Production complexity: multiple locations, permits, special equipment, animation, interviews or product demonstrations add dependencies.
  • Post-production needs: detailed graphics, sound design, accessibility versions, translation and many formats need to be included in the scope.
  • External deadlines: a launch, event, seasonal offer or media booking can make the schedule less flexible.

For a public or unusual location, include the permit and access check in pre-production; see the Montréal shoot-permit guide. If the project also includes multiple outputs, confirm the handoff requirements before shoot day.

Use examples carefully

Published production stories can show why a schedule needs to reflect the project rather than a rule of thumb. Adobe describes video production as development, pre-production, production and post-production, and notes that the work and duration vary with project scope.[1] In one Wistia in-house launch-video case, the team reported about two weeks for pre-production, a day and a half of filming and a two-to-three-day delivery buffer before the requested deadline.[2] That is one team’s example, not a market average or a promise for a different production.

Wistia also recommends including the deadline in the project specifications and working backwards to a production schedule.[3] The practical lesson is simple: get the brief, decision-makers and dependencies onto the calendar early.

Before you confirm a launch date

  • The final outputs and usage are listed in writing.
  • One owner is responsible for collecting consolidated feedback.
  • Script, brand and technical approvals have named reviewers and due dates.
  • Shoot access, participants and location requirements are confirmed.
  • The quote states review rounds, delivery milestones and what triggers a change in scope.
  • The team has time to review the final files before publishing.
