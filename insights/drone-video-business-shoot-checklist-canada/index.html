Drone Video for Business Shoots in Canada: A Planning Checklist

Check the aircraft, pilot, airspace, location and permissions before aerial footage enters the production plan.

Published November 11, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A drone can establish a property, venue, construction site or event in seconds. It also turns a normal video shoot into an aviation operation. The right question is not simply whether the shot would look good, but whether the planned aircraft, pilot, airspace, location and timing make the shot lawful and safe.

Start with the operation, not the camera move

Write down the exact location, flight area, maximum altitude, date, time, aircraft, pilot and people who may be nearby. Transport Canada says the applicable category depends on what is flown, how it is flown, where it is flown and when it is flown.[1] A wide exterior over an empty rural property is not the same operation as a flight near downtown buildings, bystanders or an advertised event.

Put the aerial idea in the business video brief early. Do not promise a drone shot in a proposal, schedule or storyboard until the feasibility check is complete.

Confirm the drone’s operating weight

Transport Canada classifies a microdrone as under 250 grams and a small drone as at least 250 grams but not more than 25 kilograms. Attached equipment counts toward the operating weight.[2]

A drone under 250 grams does not require registration or a pilot certificate, but it still must be operated safely and legally. “Micro” is not a blanket permission to enter restricted airspace, fly negligently or ignore privacy and property rules.

Match the pilot certificate to the planned flight

For drones that require certification, confirm the pilot holds the certificate that covers the intended operation. Transport Canada’s current category overview distinguishes Basic, Advanced, Level 1 Complex and special operations. Advanced privileges can include controlled airspace with air traffic control permission and operations closer to people when the aircraft and operation meet the applicable requirements.[2]

Ask for the pilot’s proposed operating category rather than accepting “licensed” as a complete answer. The certificate, aircraft capability and actual flight plan must work together.

Register and mark aircraft when required

Transport Canada says drones weighing at least 250 grams must be registered and clearly marked with their registration number. Businesses can manage drones and share account access with staff through the Drone Management Portal.[3][4]

Record the aircraft model and registration in the production file. If the planned drone changes, repeat the category and capability review instead of assuming the substitute is equivalent.

Check airspace with current official information

Transport Canada points pilots to NAV Drone and the National Research Council’s Drone Site Selection Tool to understand airspace. The site survey should identify controlled or restricted airspace, airports, heliports and other aerodromes, as well as current NOTAMs that may temporarily affect the operation.[5]

NAV CANADA says NAV Drone can schedule flights and request permission for airspace it controls. Its FAQ notes that some requests are automatic while manual reviews can take longer; authorization requests may take up to two weeks and are typically processed in one week.[6][7] Treat that as planning guidance, not a guaranteed approval time.

Separate airspace permission from location permission

Airspace authorization does not give the crew permission to occupy private property, launch from any location or disrupt public space. Confirm the owner or venue’s written approval and the permitted launch and landing area. For a Montréal shoot that affects streets, sidewalks, parking or other public property, review the separate Montréal filming permit checklist.

Other authorities may control specific land or airspace. Transport Canada notes that take-off and landing in national parks is generally prohibited unless authorized by the park superintendent, and NAV CANADA explains that permissions controlled by other authorities must be coordinated directly.[5][8]

Treat advertised events as a special case

Transport Canada identifies flying at an advertised event as a special operation that requires permission, regardless of the drone’s size.[2] Do not assume a microdrone, an event ticket or the organizer’s invitation removes that requirement.

If an aerial shot is essential to an event recap, begin the aviation review before the event schedule and deliverables are sold. Keep a ground-based establishing-shot alternative in the plan.

Conduct and document a site survey

Before the operation, identify buildings, wires, trees, roads, crowds, reflective surfaces, radio interference, emergency access and suitable areas for take-off, landing and an emergency response. Transport Canada requires pilots to survey the area and be aware of obstacles and other hazards.[1]

Set a weather decision time and define who can cancel the aerial portion. A backup shot list keeps safety decisions from becoming a negotiation after the crew is already on location.

Plan for people, privacy and accurate representation

The flight category determines how close the aircraft may operate to people, but the production also needs a practical plan for staff, clients, neighbours and passersby. Mark controlled areas, brief the ground team and avoid capturing private spaces that are not relevant to the story. Transport Canada expressly reminds pilots to respect privacy and trespassing laws.[1]

For real estate work, aerial footage should support an accurate understanding of the property rather than imply access, boundaries or amenities the listing does not include. Connect the flight plan to the real estate video shot list and the approved property facts.

Confirm records, insurance and delivery

Keep the flight plan, authorizations, location permission, aircraft information, pilot credentials, site survey and incident contacts with the production records. Transport Canada says insurance is recommended for standard categories and may be required for operations needing a Special Flight Operations Certificate.[1] A client, property owner or venue may also require insurance.

Agree on how aerial originals and final shots will be named, reviewed and archived in the business video handoff.

Drone video pre-flight checklist

  • The exact location, date, time, flight area and altitude are documented.
  • The drone’s operating weight and attached equipment are confirmed.
  • The pilot certificate matches the planned category.
  • Registration and marking are complete when required.
  • Current airspace, aerodromes and NOTAMs are checked.
  • Required airspace authorization is approved—not merely requested.
  • The owner or venue has approved take-off, landing and filming.
  • Municipal, park, event or other authority requirements are resolved.
  • The site survey identifies obstacles, people and emergency options.
  • Weather limits and a cancellation decision time are defined.
  • Privacy, property boundaries and bystander management are planned.
  • Credentials, permissions and insurance records are saved.
  • A ground-based backup shot list is ready.
