French-First Social Video for Québec Businesses: A Production Checklist

Plan scripts, on-screen text, captions and bilingual versions before the camera rolls.

Published November 3, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

For a Québec business, language is not merely a caption choice at the end of an edit. It affects the concept, speaker, graphics, framing, review process and distribution plan. A French-first workflow protects production quality while leaving room for a strong English version when the audience needs one.

Begin with the publication context

Before writing, record who is publishing the video, which products or services it promotes, where it will appear and whether the intended market includes Québec. The Office québécois de la langue française says commercial social-media publications about products or services intended for the Québec market must be in French. Its guidance explicitly includes videos, text placed inside images and permanent or temporary content.[1]

The same OQLF guidance says another language may also be used, provided the French version is at least equivalent and available under conditions that are at least as favourable.[1] That is a planning requirement: the team needs to decide what “equivalent” means for the complete viewer experience, not only the caption below the post.

Map every language-bearing element

Create a language inventory during pre-production. Include spoken dialogue, interview answers, voice-over, burned-in titles, product labels shown clearly on camera, lower thirds, calls to action, captions, thumbnails, post copy, landing-page text and downloadable material linked from the post.

This prevents a common production failure: translating the social caption while the video itself still contains English-only speech and graphics. The OQLF places responsibility for public content on the business that makes it available, including material supplied by a third party.[1]

Choose the right bilingual production model

There is no single edit structure that suits every audience. Choose deliberately among these models:

  • French master only: appropriate when the campaign is intended for a French-speaking Québec audience and no additional language version is needed.
  • Separate French and English masters: useful when the message needs natural delivery in both languages, or when text density makes a combined version hard to read.
  • One spoken-language master with translated captions: efficient for some interview-led or educational pieces, provided the French experience is complete and the publication plan has been reviewed.
  • Bilingual performance in one edit: suitable when switching languages is authentic to the speaker and audience, but it requires careful pacing and complete captions.

Do not assume that one English master with a short French post caption creates an equivalent experience. Review the full combination of audio, visuals, captions, thumbnail and next step.

Write French for the screen, not for a document

On-screen copy has to survive small mobile screens and short viewing time. Preserve the meaning of the source message, then rewrite for natural spoken or visual French rather than forcing a word-for-word translation into the same number of characters.

Lock terminology early: product names, service descriptions, job titles, locations, calls to action and recurring brand phrases. The OQLF’s Vitrine linguistique recommends clear, concise social writing that follows Québec French usage.[2] A short approved glossary keeps the video, post copy and landing page consistent.

Plan captions as part of the edit

Captions support viewers who cannot hear the audio, prefer muted playback or process written language more easily. Accessibility guidance distinguishes captions from a simple transcript: captions should convey speech and meaningful non-speech information needed to understand the content, such as speaker identification, music or sound effects.[3]

Decide whether each platform will receive closed captions, burned-in captions or both. Keep the caption file for each language tied to the correct master. Check synchronization, spelling, line breaks, speaker changes and whether important captions collide with platform controls. The separate accessible social video checklist covers contrast, sound, motion and visual information in more detail.

Capture bilingual material efficiently

If a speaker will deliver both language versions, do not treat the second one as an afterthought at the end of a long day. Group the takes by section, keep the meaning aligned and allow the speaker to sound natural in each language. Record clean room tone and a few extra reaction or process shots so editors can cover differences in sentence length.

For interviews, prepare prompts in the language the participant will use. Ask the same factual question in each version, but do not force identical wording. Document any numbers, dates, claims or proper names that must remain exact across both edits. Connect this language plan to the business video brief before the shoot.

Build graphics that can expand

Design title cards, lower thirds and calls to action with enough room for language changes. Avoid flattening text into graphics before copy approval. Keep editable templates, define safe line lengths and test both versions on a small phone screen.

If public signage or commercial advertising appears as a designed element, additional rules may apply. The Charter of the French language states that public signs and commercial advertising must be in French and permits French with another language subject to the applicable predominance rule; the current regulation explains how visual impact is assessed in covered contexts.[4][5] Ask qualified counsel or the OQLF about the specific campaign when the classification or exception is unclear.

Use a two-part approval process

Creative approval and language approval answer different questions. The creative reviewer checks story, pacing, brand and call to action. The language reviewer checks accuracy, natural usage, completeness and consistency across audio, graphics, captions and post copy.

Review the final exports, not only the script. Spoken improvisation, last-minute title changes and auto-generated captions can introduce errors after the document was approved. Save a timestamped approval that identifies the language, version, platform and date.

French-first production checklist

  • The Québec audience and publication channels are documented.
  • Every spoken, written and linked language element is inventoried.
  • The French-only, separate-master, captioned or bilingual model is selected.
  • French copy is written for natural speech and mobile viewing.
  • Terminology and calls to action are approved in each language.
  • Speakers have enough time to record each required version well.
  • Graphics can accommodate different text lengths.
  • Captions include meaningful speech and non-speech information.
  • Thumbnail, post copy and landing page are included in the review.
  • Creative and language approvals identify the exact final exports.

Keep the workflow reusable

Store approved terminology, caption styles, title templates, pronunciations and export names with the project. A repeatable system turns the next bilingual campaign into a planned production choice instead of a rush translation after picture lock. It also makes a recurring content day easier to scale; the one-shoot content guide explains how to plan a larger library from one session.
