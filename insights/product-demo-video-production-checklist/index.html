Product Demo Videos: A Production and Claims Checklist

Plan a demonstration that shows a real task clearly, supports every claim and produces useful versions for sales, web and social channels.

Published November 15, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial9 min read

A useful product demo does more than make an object look attractive. It helps a specific viewer understand a real task: what the product does, how it is used, what conditions matter and what action to take next. The production plan must protect both clarity and accuracy.

Choose one viewer and one task

Start with the person who needs the demonstration and the job they are trying to complete. A first-time customer needs different context from a distributor, installer or existing user. Define the problem, starting state, action and visible result in one sentence.

Resist turning one short demo into a catalogue of every feature. Select the task most closely connected to the viewer’s decision and document it in the business video brief. Secondary features can become separate videos or supporting cutdowns.

Build a claims sheet before the script

List every statement the video may make about performance, effectiveness, durability, speed, savings, compatibility and comparison. Next to each one, record its source, test conditions, required qualification and approver. Include claims communicated by the visuals—not only the spoken script.

The Competition Bureau says a product performance, effectiveness or lifespan claim must be supported by an adequate and proper test conducted before the claim is made. It also notes that words, images, design, metadata and online advertising can contribute to a performance claim.[1] Have qualified legal or regulatory reviewers assess claims where the product or industry requires it.

Review the general impression, not only each sentence

A technically true line can still sit inside a misleading demonstration. The Competition Bureau explains that courts consider both literal meaning and the general impression created by the full representation. A photo or illustration can make otherwise true text misleading, and missing material information can alter the impression.[2]

Review the edit as a viewer would experience it. Does a cut hide a slow step? Does the “before” condition differ from the “after” condition? Does a beauty shot show a different model or accessory? Are qualifications readable when the claim appears? Fine print should not be expected to reverse the main message.

Use realistic demonstration conditions

Record the product version, accessories, input materials, environmental conditions and setup used on camera. If the result depends on temperature, surface type, network speed, operator skill or another variable, decide what the viewer needs to know.

The Competition Bureau’s performance-claims guidance says testing should reflect real-world use and support the general impression of the marketing claim.[1] A filmed demonstration is not automatically a qualifying test. Keep the evidence and the production footage as separate records, linked by the approved claims sheet.

Storyboard the task in observable steps

Break the demonstration into the minimum steps a viewer must see: starting condition, setup, interaction, key mechanism and result. Mark which details need a wide view for context, a medium view for the user’s actions and a close-up for controls or evidence.

Write visual proof into the storyboard. If the narration says a component locks, show the lock engaging. If the user must select a setting, make that setting legible. Avoid covering the critical action with a presenter, graphic or decorative camera move.

Prepare duplicate products and consumables

Use a clean, current product that matches the version being sold. Bring duplicates when a scratch, spill, battery issue or mechanical failure could stop the shoot. Prepare enough consumables to repeat the demonstration from the true starting condition.

Photograph labels, model numbers and the approved configuration before filming. Track continuity between takes so the fill level, screen, indicator lights, assembly state and accessories do not change unexpectedly.

Control reflections, screens and small details

Product surfaces often reveal the production itself. Test lighting for glossy packaging, metal, glass and display screens before the shooting day. Clean the product carefully and assign one person to check fingerprints, dust, alignment and wardrobe reflections.

For screens, confirm refresh behaviour, brightness, sensitive data and the exact software version. Capture a real screen when practical. If a screen replacement or enlarged interface is necessary for legibility, label and approve it so the visual does not imply functionality the product lacks.

Record clean sound and useful natural detail

If the product has a meaningful click, motor, alert or texture sound, record it clearly and separately. Remove distracting room noise without making the product seem silent when sound is part of normal operation.

Keep narration concise and let the visible action carry the explanation. When a presenter appears, prepare them with the on-camera speaker checklist. Verify names, measurements and technical terms before recording voice-over.

Plan captions and visible instructions

W3C states that captions are required at WCAG Level A for prerecorded synchronized video with audio information needed to understand the content.[3] Captions should cover meaningful speech and non-speech audio, and they need a human accuracy review.

Do not rely on sound alone to signal a successful step. Pair important alerts with a visible state or text explanation. Keep on-screen instructions concise, high-contrast and within safe areas for the intended crops. Apply the complete accessible video checklist to the final versions.

Design modular endings and calls to action

The same core demonstration may need different endings for a product page, retailer, sales presentation, support centre or paid campaign. Capture a clean ending before the call to action, then create channel-specific end cards without rebuilding the complete edit.

Match the next step to the viewer’s stage: compare specifications, request a quote, find a dealer, book a demonstration or read setup instructions. Avoid vague calls to “learn more” when a more precise path is available.

Create versions from one approved master

Approve one accurate long-form master before creating short edits. Mark the essential context that must stay attached to each claim so a cutdown does not remove a qualification or change the general impression.

Plan horizontal, square and vertical compositions during filming. List captions, thumbnail stills, clean product shots, textless masters and alternate calls to action in the video deliverables checklist. A modular capture plan can also support the month-of-content workflow.

Run separate accuracy and production approvals

A beautiful edit is not necessarily an accurate one. Use an editorial reviewer for pacing, brand and clarity, and a product or compliance reviewer for version, procedure, claims and qualifications. Record the approved master, date and product version.

Watch the final file without the script in hand. Confirm that a reasonable viewer can follow the task, that text is legible on a phone and that no cut, crop, thumbnail or title changes the intended meaning. The Competition Bureau advises businesses to disclose material information clearly and warns that fine-print disclaimers may fail to change a misleading overall impression.[4]

Product demo video checklist

  • One defined audience and product task guide the demonstration.
  • Every spoken, written and visual claim appears on an approved claims sheet.
  • Performance claims are supported by appropriate evidence before filming.
  • The complete video’s general impression has been reviewed.
  • The product version, accessories and demonstration conditions are documented.
  • The storyboard shows the starting state, action and result clearly.
  • Duplicate products, power and consumables are available.
  • Labels, screens, reflections and continuity are checked between takes.
  • Meaningful product sounds and narration are recorded cleanly.
  • Captions and visible instructions are accurate and legible.
  • Channel-specific endings do not change the meaning of claims.
  • Short versions preserve essential context and qualifications.
  • Product accuracy and production quality receive separate approvals.
  • The approved master is tied to a date and product version.
