Website Hero Video: A Performance and Accessibility Checklist

Plan a useful homepage video that supports the message without slowing the page or removing user control.

Published November 13, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A hero video can show a restaurant in motion, establish a property or make a service feel tangible before a visitor reads the first paragraph. It can also obscure the headline, consume mobile data, fail to play or distract from the action the page is meant to produce. The production and the website implementation need one shared plan.

Give the video one job

Write the purpose in one sentence before building a shot list: establish the space, demonstrate a process, show the team at work or create a specific mood. A hero video is not a compressed company documentary. It sits beside a headline and call to action, so every shot should strengthen that immediate message.

Connect the purpose to the business video brief. Define the intended page, audience and next action. If the visitor must listen to understand the offer, the content probably belongs in a user-initiated video player lower on the page instead of a silent background loop.

Design the first frame as a real fallback

Choose a poster image that works before playback begins and when playback never begins. MDN’s autoplay guidance recommends displaying a placeholder when autoplay is disallowed, and browser policies can differ or change with user interaction.[1]

The poster should preserve the headline’s contrast, show the intended subject and avoid an awkward mid-action expression. Export it from the approved grade rather than asking the website team to use an arbitrary frame. Review the complete hero with the poster visible, not only with the loop running on a fast office connection.

Plan for muted autoplay—and for no autoplay

Browsers generally restrict autoplay for audible media. MDN notes that muted video or video without an audio track is not subject to the same blocking as audible media, while actual behaviour still varies by browser and context.[1]

For a decorative hero loop, build the story visually and do not rely on dialogue, a voice-over or a music cue. Ask the developer to handle a rejected playback request gracefully and keep the poster and page content useful. Never hide the main message inside the video.

Provide a meaningful motion control

WCAG Success Criterion 2.2.2 says that moving content which starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds and appears alongside other content needs a mechanism to pause, stop or hide it unless the movement is essential.[2] A hero loop behind a headline will usually be presented in parallel with other content.

Include a visible, keyboard-operable pause control in the page design. Pausing only while a pointer hovers over the video does not give every visitor reliable control. The paused state should remain paused while the person reads or navigates the page.

Create a calm loop instead of hiding the cut

A clean loop does not require constant movement. Use shots with stable beginnings and endings, avoid a rapid montage under body text and test the transition from the last frame back to the first. If the loop becomes distracting after three repetitions, simplify the edit.

A short fade or a matched movement can soften the seam, but the result still needs to support readable text. Keep faces, logos and high-contrast action away from the areas reserved for the headline and buttons.

Shoot for desktop and mobile crops

A wide desktop hero and a tall mobile screen cannot share every composition. Mark safe zones before filming, keep the essential subject near the planned crop and capture alternate framing when the scene cannot survive both layouts. Do not assume the website will merely shrink the wide file.

Deliver a wide master, a mobile-specific version when the design uses one, and the poster image. Document the intended crop, loop points and text-safe area in the video handoff checklist.

Set a performance budget with the developer

A video file competes with the page’s text, images, fonts and scripts. Agree on a target duration, dimensions, formats and maximum file size before the final export. Ask which encodes the website can serve and whether the video is loaded immediately or only when needed.

web.dev recommends techniques such as poster images and controlling preload behaviour when lazy-loading video; the exact implementation depends on whether the video is a prominent hero or content farther down the page.[3] A hero asset should not be treated exactly like an off-screen player. Test the actual page rather than judging the isolated file.

Keep essential information outside decorative video

If the hero is decorative, the page’s HTML headline and copy must carry the offer. If the video communicates essential information, provide an accessible alternative appropriate to that content. W3C’s media guidance distinguishes captions, transcripts and audio description according to what the media contains and what users need.[4]

Do not burn important text into a moving background and assume it replaces page copy. Text in video cannot function like a semantic heading, may crop on mobile and can become unreadable over changing frames.

Test the page, not just the export

Review the implemented hero on current desktop and mobile browsers, with a narrow viewport and a slower connection. Confirm that the poster appears, muted autoplay behaves as expected, the pause control works with keyboard input, the headline stays readable and the call to action remains easy to activate.

Also test what happens when the video request fails. A resilient hero still communicates the business’s value, looks intentional and lets the visitor continue immediately.

Website hero video checklist

  • The video has one documented job on one specific page.
  • The headline, offer and call to action remain HTML content outside the video.
  • The story works without audio.
  • A graded poster image works as the first frame and permanent fallback.
  • The page handles blocked or failed autoplay gracefully.
  • A visible, keyboard-operable pause or stop control is included.
  • The edit remains calm and readable through repeated loops.
  • Desktop and mobile crops have been planned and reviewed.
  • Text-safe areas are documented for the website team.
  • File formats, dimensions and a size budget are agreed before export.
  • Essential information has the required accessible alternative.
  • The final page is tested on real devices, not only in the editing suite.
