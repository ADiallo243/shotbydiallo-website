Employer Brand Videos for Small Businesses: A Practical Recruiting Plan

Show the work, people and expectations candidates want to understand—without turning a real job into a glossy promise.

Published November 25, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A recruiting video is most useful when it answers real candidate questions: What would I do? Who would I work with? What does a normal day look like? LinkedIn’s recruiting guidance recommends combining hiring-team expertise with employee stories; Canada’s Public Service Commission similarly treats video as a supplement to a job advertisement that can show the work environment and duties.[1][2] For a small business, the goal is a clear, accurate preview that helps people decide whether to learn more—not a guarantee of more applications or hires.

Start with one role and one candidate question

Choose a specific opening or role family before planning the shoot. Write down the questions you hear from applicants or new team members, then select one that a short video can answer clearly. For example: “What does a shift handoff look like?”, “Which tools will I use?”, or “How does a new team member learn the first week’s tasks?”

Keep the role details consistent with the current job posting: responsibilities, location, schedule, qualifications and application path. If those details change, update the video or make it an evergreen culture piece that does not promise a particular opening. A practical check is to have the hiring manager compare every spoken claim and on-screen detail with the approved job description before filming and again before publication.

Choose a format that shows evidence

Use a format that lets a candidate see something real. A short “day in the role” can follow a normal task from setup to handoff. A colleague introduction can explain how the team works together. A manager can describe the role’s priorities and what support a new hire receives. A workplace tour can orient people to the space, equipment or customer environment.

These are options, not interchangeable scripts. A restaurant team might show a preparation and service handoff; a professional-services firm might explain how a new colleague joins a client project; a local retailer might walk through opening tasks and customer support. Film only what reflects normal work. Avoid staged situations that imply responsibilities, equipment, benefits or schedules the role does not actually include.

LinkedIn’s own recruiting guidance suggests practical topics such as role impact, skills and growth opportunities, and recommends a simple plan, a quiet filming location and a clear next action.[1] A compact outline can be enough: one question, two or three truthful points, a visual example and an invitation to read the full posting.

Make participation comfortable and specific

Invite people who can speak from direct experience, such as the hiring manager and one or two employees in the role. Tell each participant where the video may appear, who the intended audience is, whether the final edit will include their name or title, and how review will work. Give them the questions in advance and allow them to answer in their own words.

Ask for concrete examples rather than generic praise. “I use the inventory system to prepare the morning order” helps candidates picture a task. “We have the best culture” is a broad claim that gives them little to evaluate. If an employee would rather not appear on camera, build the story around the work, location, hands-on demonstrations or a manager explanation instead of pressuring them.

For a small team, the visual plan need not be elaborate. Record the real setting, tools, safe work practices and interactions that illustrate the role. Keep customer information, screens, documents and private conversations out of frame unless their use is explicitly approved.

Plan versions for the places candidates look

A single core story can be prepared in a few useful edits: a complete version for the careers page, a shorter introduction to accompany a specific job posting, and a vertical excerpt for the social channel where your team shares hiring updates. Keep the job title, location and application link close to each version so a viewer can find the details after watching.

For each destination, decide whether the video will play on the page or link to a dedicated watch page. Google recommends a distinct, accessible page where watching one video is the main purpose when you want that video to be eligible for video search features; a video embedded in a broader page may not be considered a watch page.[3] That is a search eligibility distinction, not a promise that a recruiting video will rank or be indexed.

Use a tracked link if you want to compare clicks from different placements, and measure it alongside the job page’s normal application steps. Views or clicks alone do not tell you whether the role is a fit or whether a hire resulted. Compare only measures tied to the goal you chose, and remember that other parts of the hiring process affect outcomes.

Build accessibility and language decisions into the plan

Prepare accurate synchronized captions for prerecorded speech and meaningful sounds. W3C’s WCAG 2.2 guidance explains that captions make synchronized media understandable to people who are deaf or hard of hearing.[4] Check names, job terms and locations in the caption file, and review contrast and reading time for any on-screen text.

Decide which language versions are needed before filming. In Montréal, that can mean recording French and English interviews separately, adding subtitles, or producing two edits from the same shoot. Choose based on the audience and the employer’s communication needs; do not assume a single bilingual version will be equally clear for everyone. The federal toolkit’s bilingual production guidance applies to Government of Canada communications, not as a general legal rule for every private employer, but its planning lesson is useful: budget time to make each language version equally clear and reviewable.[2]

A practical recruiting-video workback

  1. Confirm the role: Choose the current opening, hiring audience, job facts and one candidate question.
  2. Write the outline: Select the employee or manager voices, visual proof, language versions and call to action.
  3. Confirm participation: Explain the intended uses and obtain the appropriate internal approvals before filming.
  4. Capture real work: Film a quiet interview plus relevant tasks, locations and supporting details, while protecting private information.
  5. Review for accuracy: Have the hiring manager verify that the edit matches the role and current posting; make corrections before export.
  6. Prepare delivery: Export the full version and agreed cutdowns, captions, thumbnail, transcript if needed, and destination links.
  7. Check the path: Test playback, captions, mobile layout, application link and language versions before the posting goes live.

The Canada Public Service Commission’s video recruitment toolkit recommends planning scripting, approvals, production, editing and publication as one workback, and describes employee, manager and workplace footage as useful ways to show a position.[2] Its process is designed for federal public-service recruitment; adapt the sequence to your own team rather than treating its procedures as a requirement for private businesses.

Before publishing

  • Does the video show the role as it is today?
  • Can a candidate tell which job or team it describes?
  • Are claims about schedule, responsibilities and work environment accurate?
  • Have participants reviewed how their contribution and identity will be presented?
  • Are captions, language versions and accessible playback ready?
  • Does every version point to the correct job details and application step?
