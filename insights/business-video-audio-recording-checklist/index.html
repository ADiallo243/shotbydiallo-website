Business Video Audio: A Recording and Delivery Checklist

Plan dialogue, room sound and delivery files so the finished video is clear, usable and ready for every approved channel.

Published November 22, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

Viewers may forgive a modest image, but they struggle to follow dialogue buried by echo, traffic or distorted levels. Reliable business-video audio begins before the camera rolls: define the voices that matter, listen to the location, choose a recording plan, monitor every take and deliver the right masters and access copies.

Put audio requirements in the brief

List every voice and sound the story needs: presenter, interview subject, customer, audience questions, product sounds, room atmosphere, music and voice-over. Note the filming environment, languages, intended channels and whether the final piece needs captions, a transcript or a clean version for translation.

These choices affect crew roles, microphone count, recording tracks and post-production time. They belong in the business video brief, not in an improvised decision after the location is dressed.

Listen to the location before setup

Visit or assess the room at roughly the same time the shoot will happen. Pause and listen for ventilation, refrigeration, traffic, elevators, construction, kitchen equipment, neighbouring music and reflective surfaces. A location can look quiet while producing a constant tone or echo that is difficult to remove cleanly.

Record a short sample and play it back through headphones. Decide what can be switched off safely, what can be moved, and whether the speaking position or schedule should change. Add those decisions to the location-scout checklist and call sheet.

Match the microphone to the speaker and space

Choose the microphone for the situation rather than for appearance alone. A lavalier can follow a moving speaker but may pick up clothing noise. A boom can sound natural while staying out of frame, but it needs placement and an operator. A handheld microphone can be practical in a loud event environment and makes the audio source visible to viewers.

Test the exact wardrobe, movement and camera framing before committing. If an important speaker cannot be captured consistently with one approach, plan an independent second source.

Give important voices their own tracks

Record each key speaker to an isolated track when the equipment and crew allow it. Separate tracks give the editor more control over level, noise and overlap than a single mixed recording. Keep camera or scratch audio for synchronization and reference, but do not assume it can replace a properly placed primary microphone.

Label the recorder, input and speaker consistently. At the start of a setup, identify the scene or speaker aloud and confirm that the camera and recorder clocks, file naming or synchronization method are understood.

Set levels with the real performance

Ask the speaker to deliver part of the script or answer a question at their actual volume. A quiet rehearsal does not reveal what will happen during an enthusiastic answer. Set enough margin for natural changes and watch for clipping; distorted peaks usually cannot be repaired into a clean original.

Check every active input after a microphone, battery, wardrobe or speaking position changes. A meter confirms signal, but it does not reveal every rustle, wireless hit or background sound.

Monitor continuously

Assign someone to listen through suitable headphones while recording. That person should watch for clothing contact, cable movement, radio interference, changing background noise, low batteries and a microphone that has shifted. Stop and correct a problem while the speaker, location and camera setup are still available.

A second recorder or safety track reduces risk, but redundancy is not a substitute for listening. Two systems can capture the same bad placement or room noise.

Capture room tone and useful natural sound

Record a quiet sample of the room after people settle and equipment reaches its normal state. Editors can use that consistent ambience to smooth cuts without creating unnatural silence. Also capture the meaningful sounds that help explain the scene: a machine starting, food hitting a grill, a door opening or an audience reacting.

Keep intentional natural sound separate from avoidable noise. The goal is not a silent world; it is a clear hierarchy in which dialogue and story information remain understandable.

Record and review a technical test

Before the first important take, record a short sample using the full signal path. Play it back from the recorded file rather than trusting only live monitoring. Confirm every expected channel is present, the correct microphone reaches the correct track, synchronization works and no device is applying an unintended setting.

Repeat the test after a significant reset, location move or equipment change. A one-minute check is cheaper than discovering a missing track in the edit.

Protect speech intelligibility in the edit

Build the mix around the information viewers must understand. Balance speakers, remove distractions carefully, and keep music and effects from masking words. Aggressive noise reduction can introduce new artifacts, so compare the processed result with the original and prefer a natural voice over an unnaturally polished one.

Review on more than one listening system, including ordinary headphones, a laptop or phone speaker. Listen all the way through transitions, not only the loudest sections. Confirm that translated or alternate-language versions use the approved dialogue and mix.

Create captions and transcripts from the final mix

W3C advises planning accessibility from the start. Captions should include speech and the non-speech audio needed to understand the content, while transcripts can provide the same audio information in a separate text format.[1] Generate them from the approved edit, then review names, technical terms, punctuation, timing and speaker changes.

Do not treat automatic output as finished without review. The accessible social video checklist covers readable on-screen text, captions, meaningful audio and motion as one production system.

Keep a master and make channel-specific copies

The U.S. National Archives distinguishes high-quality preservation copies from compressed access copies and recommends retaining technical metadata and checksums where appropriate.[2] For a business workflow, keep the best approved audio master and project context instead of relying only on a downloaded social-media file.

Create platform copies from that approved source. YouTube currently recommends 48 kHz audio using AAC-LC or Opus in supported uploads, with recommended bitrates that vary by channel layout.[3] Treat platform settings as destination-specific and recheck them before delivery because specifications can change.

Include audio masters, caption and transcript files, language versions, licences and approval records in the delivery plan. Archive the approved master and its documentation using a recoverable, labelled structure.

Business video audio checklist

  • Every required voice, language and meaningful sound is listed in the brief.
  • The intended channels, captions, transcripts and translation needs are known.
  • The location has been checked for steady noise, interruptions and echo.
  • Each key speaker has a microphone suited to their movement, wardrobe and setting.
  • Important voices are recorded independently where practical.
  • Camera or scratch audio is available for reference and synchronization.
  • Levels are tested with the speaker’s real delivery and enough margin for louder moments.
  • An assigned person monitors the recording continuously.
  • Batteries, media, cables, wireless frequencies and backup paths are checked.
  • A technical test is recorded and played back before important takes.
  • Room tone and purposeful natural sound are captured.
  • Tracks and files are labelled consistently.
  • The mix keeps dialogue understandable over music and effects.
  • The final mix is reviewed on common listening devices.
  • Captions and transcripts are reviewed against the approved edit.
  • The best approved master is kept separately from platform copies.
  • Current destination specifications are checked before export.
  • Masters, text files, licences and approvals are included in delivery and archive records.
