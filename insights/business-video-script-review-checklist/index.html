Business Video Scripts: A Practical Review and Approval Checklist

Review a script for clarity, accuracy, visual meaning, accessible delivery and a clean approval handoff before filming.

Published November 16, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A business video script is not only narration. It is a production document that coordinates spoken words, interview prompts, on-screen text, visuals, claims, accessibility and approval. The review process should make the video easier to understand and safer to produce—not simply make every stakeholder’s preferred phrase fit.

Return to the audience and intended action

Put the audience, problem and next step at the top of the review document. Every section should help that viewer understand something or take the intended action. If a sentence exists only because the organization wants to mention itself, ask whether it earns its place.

The Government of Canada’s content guidance recommends putting the most important task information first, keeping content brief and task-oriented, and using short sentences that communicate one idea.[1] These principles transfer well to spoken video: viewers cannot scan backwards as easily as readers can.

Review the structure before editing individual words

Read only the section headings or scene purposes. Can a reviewer follow the logic from opening to problem, explanation, proof and next step? Move, combine or remove sections before polishing sentences that may not survive.

A useful structure often answers five questions: Why should the viewer keep watching? What problem or opportunity matters? What does the business offer? What evidence makes it credible? What should the viewer do next? The exact order can change, but the progression should be deliberate.

Write for listening, not silent reading

Read every spoken line aloud at a natural pace. Shorten stacked clauses, repeated qualifications and lists that are difficult to retain by ear. Replace internal terminology with words the intended audience already uses. Preserve necessary technical meaning rather than oversimplifying it.

Accessibility Standards Canada defines plain language as wording, structure and design clear enough for the intended audience to find, understand and use the information. Its standard recommends clear, precise and familiar words, while recognizing that audience needs determine what is understandable.[2]

Give every claim an owner and source

Highlight claims about results, performance, price, savings, popularity, experience, timelines, comparisons and customer outcomes. Add a comment naming the evidence, conditions, approver and date. If a claim cannot be verified, revise or remove it before recording.

The Competition Bureau explains that materially false or misleading claims can be assessed by both their literal meaning and their general impression. Material information is information that could influence someone to buy or use the promoted product or service.[3] Reviewers should therefore evaluate the complete message rather than approving each sentence in isolation.

Review what the pictures imply

Add a visual column beside the spoken script. Describe what the viewer will see when each claim is made. An image, edit, label or demonstration can create a promise that the voice-over never states directly.

Check whether the “before” and “after” conditions match, whether a screen or product version is current, whether stock footage represents the real service, and whether a fast edit hides relevant steps. The product demo checklist provides a deeper claims review for demonstrations.

Make the call to action specific and possible

Name the next step precisely: book a consultation, request a quote, compare plans, visit a location or download a guide. Confirm that the destination exists, works on mobile and matches the promise made in the video.

Remove false urgency and unsupported scarcity. If an offer has conditions, include the material information in a place and form the viewer can reasonably understand. Do not rely on tiny or momentary fine print to reverse the main message.

Plan accessibility inside the script

Mark information conveyed only by sound and information conveyed only by the image. W3C’s media planning guidance distinguishes captions, transcripts and audio description according to whether needed information is auditory or visual.[4]

Write meaningful sound cues into the caption plan and add speaker identification where it is needed for understanding. W3C’s guidance for prerecorded captions explains that captions include dialogue as well as meaningful non-speech information.[5] Apply the full accessible social video checklist before publishing.

Separate narration, dialogue and on-screen text

Use distinct columns or styles for voice-over, interview audio, presenter lines, captions, lower thirds, supers and calls to action. This prevents a review comment on one layer from silently changing another.

Do not repeat every spoken sentence as on-screen text. Use the image and text to support comprehension: names, figures, steps, dates and the next action. Verify spelling, capitalization, units, URLs and approved brand terms in a separate text pass.

Prepare pronunciation and bilingual decisions

Flag names, places, technical terms, acronyms and words from another language. Add a phonetic note or reference recording approved by someone who knows the correct pronunciation. Do not wait for the recording session to discover uncertainty.

For bilingual production, decide whether each version is a translation, adaptation or separately written script. A line that fits in English may be longer in French, and a direct translation may not sound natural. Approve each language version and its on-screen text independently.

Time the real performance

Estimate duration by recording a natural read, not by dividing the word count by a generic speed. Pauses, demonstrations, reactions, titles and visual comprehension all use time. A presenter may also need shorter sentences than a professional voice artist.

Mark optional lines and a priority order before filming. If the final video must be shorter, the editor can remove an approved module instead of cutting necessary context from a claim.

Limit the number of approval voices

Assign one person to collect comments and resolve conflicts. Define who approves brand, subject matter, legal or compliance issues, accessibility and the final production draft. Reviewers should comment within their role and identify whether an edit is required or suggested.

Use one shared version rather than parallel attachments. Close unanswered comments, record the approval date and label the version clearly. After approval, changes that affect claims, locations, deliverables or timing should return to the responsible reviewer.

Prepare the filming draft

The filming draft should be easy to use under production conditions. Number scenes and pages; identify speakers; separate audio and visual directions; include pronunciation notes; and show approved alternatives. Remove comment history and rejected wording from the version used on set.

Share the final script with the director, producer, camera, sound, art or product lead, caption editor and client contact as appropriate. Connect it to the location scout, schedule and deliverables list so the document reflects the production that will actually happen.

Business video script review checklist

  • The intended audience and next action appear at the top of the document.
  • The structure works before individual sentences are polished.
  • Spoken lines use clear, natural language the audience understands.
  • Every material claim has evidence, conditions and an approver.
  • The general impression of words, pictures and edits is accurate.
  • The call to action is specific, working and consistent with the offer.
  • Auditory and visual information have an accessibility plan.
  • Narration, dialogue, captions and on-screen text are clearly separated.
  • Names, figures, dates, units, URLs and brand terms are verified.
  • Pronunciation and bilingual adaptations are approved.
  • Timing is based on a natural performance with visual pauses.
  • Optional modules are identified before filming.
  • Review roles and one comment owner are assigned.
  • The approved version and date are recorded.
  • The filming draft is clean, numbered and connected to the production plan.
