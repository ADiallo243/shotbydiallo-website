Business Video Files: An Archive and Backup Checklist

Keep finished masters, project records and reusable footage organized, recoverable and understandable after delivery.

Published November 18, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial7 min read

A delivered video is not the end of its useful life. The master may need a new crop next month, a caption correction next year or proof of a music licence during a platform review. A simple archive plan keeps the right files, explains what they are and creates more than one path to recovery.

Decide what the archive must support

Start with business needs, not storage capacity. Decide whether the archive must support simple republication, future social edits, full project re-editing, legal or licence checks, or long-term preservation. Those goals determine which files are essential.

Assign an archive owner and document whether the client, production partner or both retain each category. Put retention periods, deletion responsibilities and any paid storage after the agreed project period into the production agreement. An archive is a managed service, not an unlimited promise that every working file will exist forever.

Separate masters, access copies and working files

Keep the approved final master distinct from smaller viewing or social copies. The U.S. National Archives describes preservation files as high-resolution, high-bitrate and lightly compressed or uncompressed, while access copies may use lower resolution, lower bitrate and more compression.[1] For a business archive, the practical lesson is to preserve the best approved master available instead of treating a compressed social download as the master.

If future re-editing matters, also keep the project file, graphics, fonts or licence references, approved audio, captions, transcripts and the selected original footage needed to rebuild the piece. Confirm that the software versions and linked media are documented before retiring the production drive.

Preserve the original production characteristics

The Library of Congress recommends retaining file-based video at its original production resolution and frame rate, and lists high-quality master formats separately from viewing proxies such as MP4.[2] This does not mean every small business needs an institutional preservation format. It means the archive should not quietly reduce resolution, frame rate or quality just to save space.

Record the master’s container, codec, resolution, frame rate, audio layout and colour information in a manifest or media report. Keep a broadly playable access copy for quick review, but label it as a proxy or delivery version rather than the preservation master.

Use a consistent folder structure

A person unfamiliar with the edit should be able to locate the approved master, permissions and supporting files. The U.S. National Archives recommends structured directories, human-readable unique names and descriptive or technical metadata for sustained access.[3]

A compact project structure might include 01_admin, 02_releases_licences, 03_source_selected, 04_project, 05_masters, 06_platform_versions, 07_captions_transcripts and 08_archive_manifest. Use the same pattern across productions so staff do not have to relearn the archive each time.

Name files so versions remain unambiguous

Build names from stable fields such as client, project, asset, language, aspect ratio, approval state and date. For example: client-campaign-hero-en-16x9-approved-20261118.mov. Avoid calling several files final, final2 and final-revised.

The U.S. National Archives’ moving-image guidance recommends human-readable, unique names, matching file extensions and avoiding symbols or spaces.[3] A short naming guide in the archive is more useful than a complicated convention no one follows.

Keep the records that explain permitted use

Store the approved brief, final script, delivery approval, talent and location releases, music and stock licences, caption files, thumbnail source and a record of where the video was intended to appear. Link each document to the asset or version it covers.

Do not assume that possessing a media file proves the right to reuse it in a different territory, channel, term or campaign. Keep licence receipts and terms with the project, and review them before a new edit. The business video music-licensing checklist explains the questions to resolve before production and reuse.

Apply the 3-2-1 backup rule to essential files

CISA’s backup guidance describes the 3-2-1 rule as three copies of important data, on two different media types, with one copy stored offsite.[4] For a small production archive, that might mean the working archive plus a separate local backup and an offsite or cloud copy.

Two folders on the same physical drive are not two independent backups. Avoid making one account, building, device or administrator the only route to every copy. Protect cloud accounts with appropriate access controls and recovery methods, and document who can restore the archive when the usual owner is unavailable.

Verify copies instead of assuming they worked

A checksum is a file fingerprint that can be compared later to detect whether content has changed. The UK National Archives explains that a changed checksum can indicate that a file was edited or corrupted, while renaming the file alone does not change its checksum.[5]

Generate and retain checksums for important masters when your workflow supports them. More importantly, schedule a real restore test: retrieve a master and its supporting record from backup, open it, confirm audio and picture, and verify that the team can find the correct version. A backup that has never been tested is only an assumption.

Control access and review retention

Give people only the access needed for their role. Separate public delivery links from the archive, expire temporary review links and remove former collaborators when appropriate. Sensitive interview footage, personal contact information and signed releases should not inherit the same broad sharing settings as a public social export.

Review the archive on a schedule. Check storage health, account ownership, licence restrictions, software dependencies, backup success and whether the material still has a documented business or legal reason to be retained. Record intentional deletions so the team does not mistake a retention decision for data loss.

Business video archive and backup checklist

  • The archive purpose, owner, retention period and cost responsibility are documented.
  • The approved master is clearly separated from access and platform copies.
  • The best approved resolution, frame rate and audio version are retained.
  • Project files and required linked media are consolidated when future editing is expected.
  • Captions, transcripts, graphics and approved thumbnails are included.
  • Releases, licences, approvals and usage notes are linked to the assets they cover.
  • Folders and file names follow one documented convention.
  • A manifest records formats, versions and essential project context.
  • Essential files follow a 3-2-1 backup approach.
  • Backup copies do not depend on one drive, location, account or administrator.
  • Checksums are retained for important masters when practical.
  • A sample restore has been completed successfully.
  • Archive and delivery-link access are reviewed separately.
  • Account recovery and handoff procedures are documented.
  • Retention, licences and storage health are reviewed on a schedule.
