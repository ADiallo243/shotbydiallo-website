Business Video Deliverables: A Practical Handoff Checklist

Define the masters, platform versions, accessibility files and project records before the final export.

Published November 9, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial7 min read

A finished video is only useful when the business receives files it can identify, publish and reuse within the agreed rights. A clear delivery plan connects each export to a destination, gives the publishing team the supporting assets it needs and records what is—and is not—included.

Start with destinations, not a generic export list

Write down the confirmed destinations: website, YouTube, LinkedIn, Instagram, paid advertising, presentation, sales email or another channel. Then define one deliverable for each real use. The business video brief should establish the primary audience, message and placement before production begins.

Avoid promising “every format.” Platform requirements change, and different placements may need different framing, length, captions, thumbnails or calls to action. For example, LinkedIn currently accepts several video formats and lets publishers add a thumbnail and caption file, so those supporting assets should be planned when LinkedIn is a confirmed destination.[1]

Deliver a clearly identified master

The master is the approved full-quality version from which agreed publishing files are made. Its file name should identify the client or campaign, title, language, orientation, version and approval state. Keep technical terms in a short readme so a future team member does not have to guess which file is final.

Do not choose a codec or container by habit alone. Confirm where the file will be uploaded and export a supported format. YouTube, for example, publishes a current list of supported upload formats.[2] A platform-ready file is more useful than a technically impressive file the client cannot upload.

List every publishing version separately

For each version, record the destination, aspect ratio, approximate duration, language, caption treatment, thumbnail, call to action and approval owner. A typical package might include one website film, one horizontal YouTube version, two vertical social edits and one clean presentation copy. The exact package should follow the campaign, not a universal bundle.

When a crop changes the composition, review it as its own edit. Important faces, products, text and logos can be lost or covered by platform controls. LinkedIn advises keeping key elements away from the edges because its interface may overlap them.[1]

Include captions and a transcript when required

W3C guidance says captions provide a text version of speech and necessary non-speech audio for people who are Deaf or hard of hearing, while transcripts provide a text version of the audio and may also describe important visual information.[3] Plan these assets early instead of treating them as an emergency export after approval.

Agree whether captions will be open—burned into the picture—or delivered as a separate file the platform can toggle. If both are needed, name them distinctly. Review timing, speaker identification, spelling, meaningful sound cues and any language variants. The accessible social video checklist provides a fuller production review.

Prepare the thumbnail and publishing copy

A thumbnail should match the final video and remain legible at small sizes. Deliver the approved image in the sizes needed for confirmed destinations, plus the editable source only when the agreement includes it. Keep titles, descriptions, credits and calls to action in a simple handoff document rather than hiding them inside an email thread.

When a website watch page is part of the plan, include the final title, description, thumbnail URL, publication date and player information needed for accurate metadata. The video SEO checklist explains how the page, thumbnail, structured data and sitemap should describe the same video.

Separate clean and texted versions deliberately

A clean version without permanent titles can be useful for future language adaptations or revised calls to action. It is not the same as handing over all source footage or project files. State exactly which clean versions, graphics packages, raw footage or editable files are included, and whether extra preparation or storage fees apply.

If a version contains music, stock media, fonts or templates, do not assume the client can extract and reuse those elements elsewhere. The music licensing checklist explains why the intended platforms, paid uses, territory, term and alternate edits should be reviewed before delivery.

Archive rights and approvals with the media

Keep the signed scope, final approval, participant and location permissions, music or stock licences, factual approvals and any restrictions with the delivery record. Canada’s intellectual-property guidance notes that a written agreement can detail how copyright may be used.[4] The handoff should therefore state who may publish, where, for how long and whether edits or third-party reuse are permitted.

Agree on storage before the download link expires

Define how long the producer will retain masters, source footage and project files, what the client must download, and how a future restore would be handled. Confirm that the client has successfully downloaded and opened the agreed files. A transfer link is a delivery method, not a permanent archive.

Business video handoff checklist

  • Every deliverable has a named destination and purpose.
  • The approved master is clearly labelled.
  • Platform versions list orientation, duration and language.
  • Captions and transcripts are included where required.
  • Thumbnails and publishing copy match the final video.
  • Clean, texted and language versions are distinguished.
  • Raw footage and editable project files are explicitly included or excluded.
  • Music, stock, participant and location records are archived.
  • Usage rights and restrictions are written down.
  • File names identify version and approval state.
  • A readme explains the package.
  • Retention, backup and restore terms are confirmed.
  • The client has tested the final files before the delivery window closes.
