Business Video Brief: 12 Questions to Answer Before Filming

Turn a vague request for “a video” into a production the team can approve and use.

Published November 2, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

A useful brief is a decision document, not a collection of adjectives. It tells the producer who the video is for, what the audience should understand, which claims are supported, where the work will appear and who can approve it. Answering these 12 questions before filming reduces avoidable rewrites without pretending that every creative decision can be made in advance.

1. What business outcome should the video support?

Choose one primary job: explain a service, introduce the team, support recruitment, document an event, answer a common question or give a sales conversation a clearer starting point. “Increase awareness” is too broad unless the team defines what the intended audience should notice or do.

Write the outcome as a useful change: “A prospective client understands our three-step process and knows how to request a consultation.” That sentence gives the script and call to action something concrete to serve.

2. Who is the specific audience?

Describe the viewer by situation rather than demographics alone. What do they already know? What are they uncertain about? Where are they likely to see the video? A first-time visitor on a service page needs different context from an existing customer watching a short update.

3. What is the one message they should remember?

Write a single sentence without production language. If the brief contains five equal messages, decide which one leads and which ones become supporting points or separate videos. A clear hierarchy makes the opening, examples and ending easier to design.

4. What evidence supports the message?

List demonstrations, verified facts, documents, customer experiences or visual proof that can appear on screen. Separate a subjective description from an objective performance claim. The Competition Bureau says performance claims must be based on an adequate and proper test completed before the claim is made, and warns marketers not to distort the scope of testimonials.[1]

Mark every number, comparison and “best,” “fastest” or “guaranteed” statement for factual review. A creative brief is not evidence by itself.

5. Which platforms and placements are confirmed?

Name the actual destinations: website hero, service page, LinkedIn post, Instagram Reel, TikTok ad, YouTube Short, presentation or sales email. Do not use “social media” as one format. TikTok’s current creative guidance recommends 9:16 vertical framing, resolution of at least 720p, space for interface elements and a hook-body-close structure for ads.[2]

The brief should identify which placement is primary and which versions are adaptations. That prevents a wide master from becoming an unreadable vertical crop after the shoot.

6. What deliverables are actually needed?

For each deliverable, record its purpose, orientation, approximate duration, language, captions, call to action and owner. A practical list might include one website film, three vertical excerpts and a clean interview clip. Avoid ordering “all the raw footage plus every possible version” without a use plan.

If the goal is a reusable monthly library, connect the brief to the one-shoot content planning guide and define the repeatable content categories before filming.

7. Who or what must appear?

List speakers, customers, employees, products, locations, demonstrations and brand elements. Confirm availability and identify who can authorize participation and use. For a Québec business, personal-information collection should start with a legitimate, important and real objective and be limited to what is necessary for that purpose, according to the Commission d’accès à l’information.[3]

Do not collect extra personal details simply because the interview form has room for them. The appropriate permission and privacy process depends on the project; obtain qualified advice when needed.

8. What locations and actions tell the story?

Choose spaces because they explain the work, not only because they look impressive. Note access windows, noise, customers, confidential information, safety restrictions, power, parking and weather alternatives. Then list the real actions the camera needs: greeting a client, preparing a product, demonstrating a process or moving through a space.

9. What must be accessible from the start?

Plan captions, readable on-screen text, meaningful audio and any required description while scripting and storyboarding. W3C guidance identifies accessibility considerations throughout planning, scripting, recording and production, including visual information that is not described and text with insufficient contrast.[4]

Use the separate accessible social video checklist for a more detailed review. Accessibility added only after picture lock may require avoidable redesign or re-editing.

10. What brand and legal constraints apply?

Attach the current logo files, name styling, colours, terminology and approved calls to action. Also identify music, location, talent, customer, product and third-party content rights that need review. Record prohibited claims, confidential details and industry-specific approval requirements rather than expecting the editor to discover them later.

11. Who approves facts, creative and publication?

Name one owner for consolidated feedback. If different people approve factual accuracy, brand, legal issues and final publication, document each role and the order of review. Decide how many review rounds are included and what counts as a scope change.

A timestamped note such as “approved for website and organic LinkedIn use” is more useful than an isolated message saying “looks good.” For customer-led content, use the testimonial consent and accuracy checklist.

12. What are the schedule, budget and non-negotiables?

List the publication date, filming window, decision deadlines and any event or seasonal dependency. Separate fixed requirements from preferences. If the budget cannot support every location, speaker and deliverable, prioritize the assets most closely tied to the primary outcome.

Copy-and-fill brief

  • Primary outcome: The video should help…
  • Audience: The main viewer is…
  • Core message: After watching, they should remember…
  • Evidence: We can support that message with…
  • Primary placement: The main destination is…
  • Deliverables: We need…
  • People and products: The production must include…
  • Locations and actions: The story happens through…
  • Accessibility: We will plan for…
  • Constraints and rights: The team must respect…
  • Approvals: Facts, creative and publication are approved by…
  • Schedule and budget: The fixed boundaries are…

Final brief check

  • The brief names one primary outcome and audience.
  • The main message fits in one sentence.
  • Objective claims have an evidence owner.
  • Each deliverable has a destination and purpose.
  • Orientation and platform needs are decided before filming.
  • Necessary people, locations and permissions are identified.
  • Accessibility is part of the plan, not an export task.
  • One person will consolidate feedback.
  • The publication date leaves time for factual and final review.
  • Nice-to-have ideas are separated from non-negotiables.
