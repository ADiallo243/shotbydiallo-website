How to Compare Video Production Quotes in Montréal: A Practical Budget Guide

A fair comparison starts with the work included. Use this scope-first checklist to understand what each proposal covers before comparing totals.

Published November 23, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial7 min read

Two video proposals can show very different totals because they describe different projects. One may include a full pre-production process, a two-person crew and several edited versions; another may price a shorter shoot and one master edit. Before deciding which quote is “cheaper,” make the deliverables, assumptions and exclusions comparable.

Ask for the same brief and outputs

Send each producer one clear brief: audience, purpose, where the video will appear, approximate runtime, locations, people on camera, required languages, deadline and the final assets you need. A business video brief helps make those assumptions visible.

List each requested output separately. For example: one 60-second website film, three vertical cutdowns, French and English caption files, and a clean master without burned-in text. “A video” can mean one finished file or a package of platform versions; the quote should say which.

Compare these scope categories line by line

  • Pre-production: concept development, scripting, research, location scouting, scheduling, shot planning and project coordination.
  • Shoot: number of production days or hours, crew roles, camera and lighting package, audio, locations, travel, setup and overtime assumptions.
  • People and locations: talent, presenters, makeup, props, set dressing, location fees, permits and any client-provided resources.
  • Post-production: editing, colour, sound mix, motion graphics, music, captions, language versions, aspect ratios and number of revision rounds.
  • Usage and handoff: where and how long you may use the finished work, whether paid advertising is included, and whether raw footage, project files, transcripts or separate audio are supplied.
  • Schedule and payment: milestones, client review windows, deposit, balance, rescheduling terms and how added work is approved.

For a deliverables handoff, see the business video deliverables checklist. For music, clarify rights and permitted uses in writing; this Canadian music licensing checklist explains the questions to raise.

Make exclusions and revision rules explicit

Ask what is not included: extra locations, additional shoot time, talent fees, travel beyond a defined area, rush editing, new versions after approval, translations, paid-media usage, raw footage or platform upload. An exclusion is not automatically a problem; it becomes a budgeting surprise when nobody knows it exists.

“Unlimited revisions” can sound reassuring but still leave both sides unsure about what counts as a revision. Ask how many review rounds are included, who consolidates feedback, what a round covers and how a change in direction is priced. A clear script and approval workflow can reduce avoidable late changes.

Use a simple comparison worksheet

Copy this checklist into a spreadsheet or proposal review. Mark each item included, excluded or unclear for every quote:

  • Number of shoot days, hours and locations
  • Crew roles and included equipment
  • Pre-production and client responsibilities
  • Exact finished videos, runtimes, aspect ratios and language versions
  • Captions, graphics, music and accessibility files
  • Included review rounds and approval deadlines
  • Usage rights, term, territory and paid-ad permissions
  • Raw footage and project files, if needed
  • Travel, permits, talent, overtime and other pass-through costs
  • Payment milestones, delivery date and change-order process
  • Applicable taxes and whether the quoted total includes them

Then calculate: comparable project total = quoted production fee + required add-ons + applicable taxes. If every package produces the same number of approved, usable outputs at a similar scope, you may also compare total divided by output count. That is only a planning comparison—not a measure of creative quality or a universal market rate.

Check the tax treatment on the invoice

For taxable supplies in Québec, Revenu Québec lists the general GST rate as 5% and the general QST rate as 9.975%; its basic rules explain that QST is calculated on the selling price excluding GST.[1] Whether a particular service is taxable and whether a supplier must collect the taxes depends on the applicable rules and the supplier’s circumstances, including registration requirements.

As a math illustration only, if both taxes apply to a $1,000 pre-tax service price, GST would be $50 and QST would be $99.75, for a total of $1,149.75. Revenu Québec says taxable sales invoices must show GST and QST separately or clearly indicate that the taxes are included.[2] Ask the producer to state whether tax is included and request a written, itemized invoice. Confirm your own tax treatment with a qualified bookkeeper or tax professional.

Choose the proposal that fits the job

Once the scope is aligned, assess the work itself: does the producer understand your audience, show relevant examples, explain the process and communicate clearly about approvals? A lower total may be the right choice when it covers what you need. A higher total may make sense when it includes important work that another proposal leaves out. The point is to decide with the same project in view.
