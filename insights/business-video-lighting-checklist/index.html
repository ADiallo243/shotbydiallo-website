Business Video Lighting: A Practical Shoot Checklist

Plan consistent, camera-ready light for people, products and business locations before the first important take.

Published November 22, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial8 min read

Good lighting does more than make a frame brighter. It guides attention, keeps people recognizable, represents products honestly and helps shots from different moments feel like the same story. The right plan starts with the location and business goal, then controls what the camera records.

Define what the light must communicate

Start with the audience, message and subject. A founder interview may need a natural, trustworthy look; a product demonstration may need even illumination and precise detail; a restaurant piece may need to preserve atmosphere without hiding the food. Write the intended mood, brand constraints and required shots into the video brief.

Decide what must remain visually accurate. Note brand colours, product finishes, uniforms, skin tones, screens, labels and any compliance-sensitive detail. This prevents an attractive setup from changing information the audience needs.

Survey the existing light

During the location scout, observe windows, overhead fixtures, signs, screens and reflective surfaces at the time filming is expected. Look for daylight that moves across the room, lights controlled by shared systems and spaces where fixtures cannot be switched off. Check whether the production can place stands safely without blocking exits or normal operations.

Photographs alone do not reveal every lighting problem. Record a short camera test and review it on a reliable display. The business video location-scout checklist covers access, power, sound, weather and other constraints that affect the setup.

Choose one motivated direction

Give the main subject a clear source direction that makes sense within the room. A window, practical fixture or controlled production light can motivate the look. Position and shape the light before adding more fixtures. Moving a source, changing its angle or bringing it closer can matter more than adding raw output.

Use fill or negative fill to control contrast, and separation or background light only when it supports the scene. The goal is not to illuminate every surface equally; it is to make the subject and important information readable while keeping the location believable.

Light faces for the person, not a formula

Seat or stand the speaker first, then adjust the light while viewing the actual camera image. Check eyes, glasses, skin highlights, hair, wardrobe and the background together. Make small changes to height, angle, diffusion and distance rather than forcing every speaker into one fixed diagram.

If multiple people appear, confirm each face remains consistent when they turn toward one another. For interviews, leave space for natural movement and make sure the chosen lighting still works at the edge of that range. Pair the setup with the on-camera interview checklist.

Control colour at the source and in camera

Mixed daylight, office fixtures and production lights can create different colour casts across a face or product. ARRI’s lighting handbook recommends balancing the camera for the light in the subject area and correcting production fixtures when they need to match ambient sources.[1]

Set and record the camera’s white-balance and tint decisions for each setup. ARRI’s image-science guidance explains that colour temperature moves the white point between blue and red, while tint adjusts between green and magenta; a neutral target, waveform or vectorscope can help reveal a cast that is hard to judge by eye.[2] Do not assume two fixtures set to the same number will match perfectly on camera—test the actual combination.

Set exposure around the important information

Expose for the subject and delivery goal, then inspect highlights and shadows that carry meaning. Check pale clothing, white packaging, windows, reflective logos, dark hair and product texture. Use the camera’s monitoring tools consistently rather than relying only on a screen whose brightness changes with the room.

Record the exposure approach for repeat setups. If the project uses a log or other production colour workflow, confirm that the monitoring view, recording settings and post-production plan agree before the shoot. An unverified look on a monitor is not the same as a finished grade.

Test practical and LED lights for flicker

Some fluorescent, LED, sodium and other sources can produce flicker, banding or colour changes that are more visible to the camera than to the eye. Sony explains that these effects depend on the light’s cycle and the way the camera exposes the sensor, and recommends testing and using supported anti-flicker or variable-shutter controls where appropriate.[3]

Review recorded clips at normal speed and frame by frame, especially when using dimmed LEDs, illuminated signs, screens or practical fixtures. Do not prescribe one universal shutter setting: mains frequency, frame rate, fixture electronics and camera controls all matter. If the source cannot be made reliable, replace it, change its output method or remove it from the shot.

Manage windows and changing daylight

Choose whether windows are part of the composition, a light source or a variable to control. Plan for the sun’s movement, passing clouds and reflections from traffic. Sheers, flags, neutral-density material, a different camera angle or a revised schedule may create more consistency than adding stronger fixtures.

For a long interview, capture a short reference at the start and watch the background between answers. If daylight changes materially, pause and correct it rather than letting the edit alternate between visibly different conditions.

Check products, screens and reflective surfaces

Move the light and camera while watching the reflection, not only the object. Bottles, polished metal, glass, screens and glossy packaging often reveal the source shape and crew position. Use larger diffusion, flags, angle changes or controlled reflections to describe shape without hiding labels or creating false product qualities.

For screens, verify brightness, refresh behaviour, legibility and permission to show the content. Record an alternate clean screen or insert when the display may change later. For demonstrations, keep hands and the task area readable throughout the full action.

Keep lighting safe and operational

Route and secure cables, use stable stands and appropriate weights, keep hot fixtures clear of people and materials, and protect exits and customer paths. Confirm circuit capacity and approved power access with the location. Assign responsibility for any overhead rigging or specialized electrical work to qualified people.

Add setup time, access notes, power requirements and weather contingencies to the production call sheet. A lighting plan only works if it can be installed, operated and removed safely within the location’s schedule.

Record continuity references

Before changing a setup, capture a reference still and note fixture position, height, output, colour settings, modifiers, camera settings and time of day. Mark stand positions when permitted. These records make pickups and multi-day shoots more repeatable and help the editor understand intended differences between scenes.

Review a representative clip with the intended grade or viewing transform before moving on. Check the subject, background, brand colours and any practical lights together. Pair picture review with the business video audio checklist so the technical test covers the complete scene.

Review for accessibility and delivery

Lighting effects that flash rapidly can create an accessibility risk. W3C’s guidance says content should not flash more than three times in any one-second period unless it remains below defined thresholds; material that does flash needs further evaluation.[4] Review strobes, practical flicker and rapidly alternating edits as part of the final accessibility check.

Grade and approve on a suitable display, then review the actual platform copy after encoding. Keep the approved master, colour workflow notes and delivery versions together. If HDR is part of the plan, use a verified HDR production and metadata workflow rather than simply labelling a bright image as HDR.

Business video lighting checklist

  • The intended mood, audience and visually critical information are in the brief.
  • Brand colours, product finishes, labels and wardrobe have been identified.
  • The location has been checked at the expected filming time.
  • Windows, overhead fixtures, signs, screens and reflective surfaces are mapped.
  • The main light has a clear direction that fits the space.
  • Faces remain readable through the speaker’s natural movement.
  • Glasses, skin highlights, wardrobe and background are checked together.
  • Mixed-light sources have been tested on the actual camera.
  • White balance, tint and exposure decisions are recorded.
  • Important highlights and shadows retain the information the story needs.
  • Practical, fluorescent and LED sources have been recorded and reviewed for flicker.
  • Changing daylight has a control or scheduling plan.
  • Products and screens are checked for reflections, glare and legibility.
  • Cables, stands, heat, power and exits are managed safely.
  • A reference still and lighting notes are captured before each reset.
  • A representative recorded clip is reviewed before the setup changes.
  • Flashing content is included in the accessibility review.
  • The final platform copy is checked after encoding.
