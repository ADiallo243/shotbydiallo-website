On-Camera Business Interview Preparation Checklist

Prepare the message, speaker, setting, permissions and publishing assets before the interview begins.

Published November 10, 2026Shot By Diallo editorial7 min read

A strong business interview should sound like a person explaining something they understand—not someone reciting a page of marketing copy. The preparation process should give the speaker a clear purpose, accurate talking points and a comfortable setting while leaving room for a natural answer.

Define the interview’s one job

Decide what the interview must help the audience understand or do. A founder story, service explanation, recruiting film and expert commentary need different questions. Connect the interview to the audience and outcome defined in the business video brief.

Write one sentence that describes the useful result: “A prospective client understands how our three-step process reduces uncertainty before the first meeting.” That sentence gives the interviewer a filter for every question and follow-up.

Choose the right speaker for the subject

Select someone with direct knowledge of the topic, not simply the most senior title. Confirm what the person can discuss, which claims need review and what information must remain confidential. For a customer story, use the separate testimonial consent and accuracy checklist.

Explain where the interview may appear and how it may be edited. In Québec, the Commission d’accès à l’information says that necessity should be assessed before seeking consent and that the objective for collecting personal information should be legitimate, important and real. Its guidance also says written consent requests should be presented separately and in clear terms.[1][2] The appropriate process depends on the project; obtain qualified advice when needed.

Send topics, not a script to memorize

Share the purpose, audience, approximate duration and five to seven topic prompts before the shoot. Let the speaker prepare facts, examples and terminology. Avoid asking them to memorize complete paragraphs: a small wording change can then feel like failure, and the delivery often becomes less natural.

Useful prompts invite a complete answer: “What happens after a client books?” “What do people misunderstand before they call?” “Can you describe one concrete example?” Ask the speaker to include enough context that the answer can stand alone after the question is removed.

Separate verified facts from personal perspective

Mark dates, numbers, comparisons, guarantees and performance claims for review. Canada’s Competition Bureau says performance claims should be supported by adequate and proper testing completed before the claim is made.[3] An interview format does not make an unsupported advertising claim safer.

Create a short fact sheet and assign one person to approve it. The speaker can still use natural language, but the editor should know which statements require exact wording, evidence or legal review.

Prepare the room and the schedule

Choose a quiet space with a controllable background, enough depth behind the speaker and limited interruptions. Turn off avoidable notifications, machinery and ventilation only when it is safe to do so. Schedule more time than the final interview length: setup, sound checks, warm-up answers and pauses are part of the production.

Ask about accessibility, mobility, sensory and communication needs in advance. Offer water, breaks and a clear explanation of where to look. The speaker should know that they can pause, restart an answer or ask for a question to be rephrased.

Plan clothing for the actual frame

Recommend clothing that feels appropriate to the speaker and brand, fits comfortably while seated and does not distract from the message. Avoid making universal rules about colour or patterns; the background, lighting and camera tests determine what works. Bring a backup option when the interview is important or the wardrobe may conflict with the set.

Start with low-pressure questions

Begin with the speaker’s name, role and a familiar part of the work. Move from simple description to opinion, example and reflection. Listen for a useful phrase and ask a focused follow-up instead of racing through the list.

If an answer is too long, ask for a shorter version with one idea. If it is too abstract, ask for a specific moment or demonstration. Do not feed a conclusion the speaker does not believe; the goal is clarity, not forced enthusiasm.

Record clean answers and useful pauses

Ask for complete sentences when the edit will remove the interviewer. Leave a brief pause before and after each answer. Repeat names, technical terms or important figures cleanly when pronunciation or accuracy may be unclear. Capture room tone and relevant b-roll that supports what the speaker actually said.

Plan captions, transcript and platform framing

W3C guidance recommends planning accessibility requirements—including captions, description and transcripts—from the start of an audio or video project.[4] Confirm who will prepare and review captions, speaker names, technical terms and language versions.

When LinkedIn is a destination, its current publishing tools support thumbnails and caption files, and its guidance recommends keeping important text and logos away from interface-covered edges.[5] Frame and light the interview for the confirmed horizontal, square or vertical edits rather than relying on an improvised crop.

Review meaning, not just appearance

The approval copy should preserve the speaker’s intended meaning and the factual context. Check titles, names, numbers, subtitles and translated text. A polished edit should not combine separate statements in a way that creates a new claim.

Include the final approved interview, caption files, transcript, thumbnail and rights records in the business video handoff.

Interview preparation checklist

  • The interview has one audience and one purpose.
  • The chosen speaker has direct knowledge of the subject.
  • Intended uses and permissions are explained clearly.
  • Topics and prompts are shared without requiring memorization.
  • Claims, dates and numbers have an evidence owner.
  • The room, schedule and accessibility needs are confirmed.
  • The speaker knows they can pause and restart.
  • Wardrobe is tested against the real set.
  • Questions move from familiar facts to useful examples.
  • Answers include enough context to stand alone.
  • Captions, transcript and language versions have owners.
  • Each planned crop protects faces, text and logos.
  • The final edit is reviewed for accuracy and intended meaning.
